The Reading Check

Use it at every stage, any time you write, say or decide something others will interpret. Thirty seconds, three moves.

Move 2 · How could it be read?

As I intended

Partly: accept some, resist some

Against me: the worst fair reading

Move 3 · What closes the gap? Pick what you'll do.

The Framing Ledger · one row per framing decision, filled in as you go

DecisionWhat we meanOther readingsEvidenceWho decidedHow we'll know it was right
The research behind it

Stuart Hall (1973) showed that a sender encodes a message and an audience decodes it, and the two never fully match. He named three readings: the intended one, a negotiated one and an oppositional one. Karl Weick (1995) showed that teams make meaning together through sensemaking, and Gioia and Chittipeddi (1991) showed that leaders shape that meaning through sensegiving. The three gap-closers are your midterm fixes: reason with the rule, say it first, name the cost.

The Brief X-Ray

A brief is a request, not a problem. Look under the surface before you agree to solve it.

Your reframe, assembled The client asked for because . The brief assumes . It also affects , who could lose . So the real problem may be .

How it worked on Netflix

Netflix answered the position, "stop password sharing," and got compliance: 5.9 million members added in one quarter.

It never answered the losses. Families heard "Love is sharing a password" in 2017 and were billed for it in 2023. That is the absent viewer.

Activity: flip the anchor

Read the brief's solution out loud. Then say its opposite. If the opposite sounds absurd, the anchor is probably doing your thinking for you.

The research behind it

Donald Schön (1983): problem setting comes before problem solving. Wedell-Wedellsborg (2017): teams solve the wrong problem when they skip reframing. Tversky and Kahneman (1974): first ideas anchor judgment; Mussweiler, Strack and Pfeiffer (2000): considering the opposite weakens the anchor. Fisher and Ury (1981) and Malhotra and Bazerman (2007): asking why behind a position reveals the interest.

The Frame Test

A frame is a best guess about the problem. Map it like an argument and test it out loud before anyone builds on it.

How it worked on Netflix

Inside Netflix, artwork personalization was framed as helping members find a show in one second.

The rival frame, "Netflix is choosing what I see without telling me," is what viewers landed on in 2018, when some felt targeted by race. Testing the rival early points straight to your fix: say it first, with a "Picked for you" label.

Activity: steelman swap

Two people argue each other's frame for two minutes. Whoever can state the rival frame best earns the right to reject it.

The research behind it

Kees Dorst (2011, 2015): design framing as "if we look at the problem as X, then Y should work," a form of abduction. Stephen Toulmin (1958): an argument has a claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier and rebuttal. Tversky and Kahneman (1981): wording changes choices. Amy Edmondson (1999): teams learn only when people feel safe to question, which decides whether a junior can challenge a frame.

to 03 The rival frame becomes the thing your research has to check.

Logic check · tick each once you've ruled it out

Your recommendation, answer first We recommend so that . Our strongest evidence is . We are less sure about . We would change our minds if .

How it worked on Netflix

A Jefferies survey said 62% would quit rather than pay (rung 2). Pilots and real sign-ups said otherwise (rungs 3 and 4). What people did beat what they said.

The evidence was strong. The gap: Netflix gave the forward-looking reason, "it hurts our ability to invest in shows," to investors and not to viewers.

The research behind it

From class: evidence that raises trust is tangible, documented and hard to dispute. Research on the say-do gap shows stated intentions are weaker than observed behavior. Gary Klein (2007): the premortem reduces overconfidence. Jay Heinrichs (Thank You for Arguing): future-focused, "deliberative" talk moves decisions. Barbara Minto: answer first. Petty and Cacioppo (1986): engaged audiences weigh arguments, busy ones lean on cues.

The Thread Check

When a concept becomes a product, the reason behind it often disappears. Follow the thread through five pins. Every unchecked pin is a break.

Visual rhetoricStorytellingCommitment and consistencyUse when: the concept turns into the product
Can the team say it in one line?
Matches what an outsider says it's for
The main visual carries it before words
What we built it for

How it worked on Netflix

Netflix artwork is visual rhetoric at its purest: one image, one second, no words. The thread was "help each viewer find a show they'll enjoy."

It broke when some images showed a minor character as if they were the lead. Measuring the watch, not the click, protected the business but not the meaning.

The research behind it

From class: visual rhetoric puts emotional, symbolic images first, with little or no text. Kurnoff and Lazarus (Everyday Business Storytelling): data and visuals land when tied to a clear story for a specific audience. Robert Cialdini (Influence): once people commit, they want to stay consistent. Stuart Hall's readings explain why an outsider reads the work differently.

to 05 The thread becomes the criteria the review judges against.

The Reasoned Review

Review the reasoning, not just the look. Hand over the reasons, not just the files. Every review is a lesson in judgment for whoever is newest.

ArgumentationPrincipled negotiationHanlon's RazorCoachingUse when: reviewing, giving feedback, handing over

The handover note

Activity: Hanlon's flip

When the next team gets it wrong, read the card, then tap to flip.

The first thought"They didn't care."Tap to flip
The better first question"What didn't they know?"Then fix the record, not the person.

How it worked on Netflix

Two reversals, paid sharing and ads, and zero acknowledgements. The reasons existed but never reached viewers, so viewers decided for themselves: "They meant it."

Silence doesn't leave people undecided. It decides for them.

The research behind it

Collins, Brown and Newman (1989), cognitive apprenticeship: experts teach judgment by modeling their thinking out loud, coaching, and asking learners to articulate and reflect. Fisher and Ury (1981): separate the people from the problem and judge by objective criteria. Patrik Meyer (The 4 Pillars of Critical Thinking): Hanlon's Razor. Edmondson (1999): people speak up only when it feels safe.

to 06 The handover note becomes your evidence of what the thinking was worth.

The Value Case

When AI makes production fast, hours stop being a fair measure. Price the thinking, and treat that new model as an innovation you have to pitch.

How it worked on Netflix

In the Warner Bros. bid, the co-CEOs called the deal "a nice to have at the right price, not a must have at any price." Knowing the walk-away was the argument.

Your "name the cost" fix adds reciprocity: give something back so the value conversation feels fair. Check the deal figures against your sources before using them.

The research behind it

Everett Rogers (1962): people adopt something new when they see advantage, fit, simplicity, a way to try it and visible results. Malhotra and Bazerman (Negotiation Genius): know your BATNA, and put several issues on the table to create value. Cialdini (Influence): people want to return what they've received. Aristotle's ethos, via Heinrichs: credibility comes from track record.

How it connects to the thesis

The templates sit across the three gaps. Where circles overlap, one template closes two gaps at once. The Framing Ledger sits at the center because every gap depends on reasons being written down.

Authority

01 makes questioning the brief a normal step. 02 asks the least senior person first. 05 has the senior ask before telling.

Judgment

02 and 03 replace seniority and taste with shared standards: Toulmin's structure and the ladder. 04 checks the work against the frame.

Capability

05 turns every review into guided practice, standing in for coaching that gets cut. 06 sits outside: it protects the time all three need.
